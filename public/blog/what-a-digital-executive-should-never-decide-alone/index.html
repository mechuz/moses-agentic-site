What a digital executive should never decide on its own

November 27, 2026 By Moses Agentic Data, risk and compliance ai guardrails for business

Most owners who are curious about a digital C-suite ask the same first question, and it is a good one: "What happens if it gets something wrong?" The honest answer is that it will, sometimes, the same way a new hire does. The useful question is not whether a digital executive makes mistakes. It is which decisions it is allowed to make on its own, which ones it can only prepare, and which ones it should never touch.

This article draws those lines in plain terms. It is written for an owner or manager of a business with somewhere between 5 and 200 people, where one bad payment, one wrong promise to a customer or one careless email can cost real money and real trust.

Start with a simple rule: prepare, do not decide

A digital executive is very good at the work that comes before a decision. It can read a stack of invoices, compare this month with last month, draft the reply to a tenant, pull every open item from a deal file and lay out the options with their numbers. That is most of the hours in a week.

The decision itself is a different thing. A decision commits the business: it spends money, makes a promise, changes a price, signs a name or speaks for the company in public. Those belong to a person with the authority to make them and the accountability when they go wrong.

This is not a new idea invented for software. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (released January 26, 2023), a voluntary federal guide for any organization that uses these tools, puts "Govern" at the center of its four functions and asks organizations to define who is responsible for oversight of each system. In small-business language: write down who decides what, before anything goes live.

Why the line matters more than the tool

Three plain facts explain why owners should be strict here.

The tools can sound sure and still be wrong

Federal guidance names this problem directly. The NIST Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024) lists "confabulation" among the main risks of these systems: content that is stated with confidence but is wrong. A draft that is wrong is cheap to fix. A payment or a promise that is wrong is not.

Money moved by email is already a target

The FBI's 2024 Internet Crime Report (published April 2025) counted 21,442 business email compromise complaints in 2024, with reported losses of about $2.77 billion for that year alone. These are schemes where someone pretends to be a vendor, a client or a boss and asks for a payment or a change of bank details. Any system that can read email and also move money is exactly what those schemes are built to fool. A person must stand between the request and the payment.

Regulators judge the claim, not the technology

The Federal Trade Commission has been clear that there is no special treatment for anything labeled AI. Its business guidance "Keep your AI claims in check" (February 27, 2023) warns that claims about what a product can do must be true and supported, and its Operation AI Comply announcement (September 25, 2024) brought cases against companies that oversold what their tools could do, including one that claimed it could stand in for a lawyer. If a digital executive writes your marketing, your business owns every word it publishes.

Seven decisions a digital executive should never make alone

Here is the list we use with owners. Every item can be prepared by a digital executive. None should be finished by one.

  1. Spending money. Paying a bill, releasing a wire, approving a refund, changing a vendor's bank details. The digital executive can match the invoice to the purchase order, flag the duplicate and queue the payment. A person releases it, and any change to bank details is confirmed by phone with a number you already had, not one from the email.
  2. Setting or changing prices. Rent increases, quotes, discounts, fee waivers. It can show you the comparison and the history. You choose the number.
  3. Signing or agreeing to contracts. It can read a lease or a vendor agreement, list the dates, renewals and unusual clauses, and compare it with your last one. It never accepts terms, and anything legal goes to your attorney.
  4. Hiring, firing and discipline. It can organize applications, schedule interviews and summarize notes. Decisions about people stay with people, and so does the conversation.
  5. Speaking to the press or posting on a sensitive matter. Routine posts can follow an approved plan. Anything about a complaint, an incident, a lawsuit or a public controversy is written or approved by ownership before it goes out.
  6. Legal, tax and medical judgments. It can gather documents for your accountant or attorney and point out what looks different from last year. It does not give advice or make the call, and it should never tell a customer or resident what to do about their health, taxes or legal position.
  7. Granting access or sharing private data. Adding a user to a system, sending a file of customer or employee records, or connecting a new app to your accounts. These are approved by the person responsible for that data.

What it can safely do on its own

None of this means a digital executive should sit idle until someone clicks a button. A large share of the work is safe to run without a sign-off, as long as it is reversible and stays inside the business:

  • Reading, sorting and summarizing documents, email and reports
  • Reconciling numbers and flagging anything that does not match
  • Drafting replies, reports, listings and posts for a person to review
  • Keeping trackers current: leads, open tasks, renewals, deadlines
  • Sending reminders to your own team
  • Preparing the weekly package an owner reads before making decisions

The test is simple. If the action can be undone in a minute and nobody outside the business sees it, it can usually run on its own. If it spends, promises, publishes or shares, a person approves it.

How to set the lines in your business

You do not need a policy binder. A one-page table is enough to start.

1. List the actions, not the job titles

Write down what the digital executive will actually do: "match invoices," "draft tenant replies," "update the deal tracker." Next to each, mark one of three columns: does it alone, prepares it for approval, or never touches it.

2. Name the approver for each one

Every "prepares it for approval" line needs a person's role next to it: the owner, the controller, the property manager. If nobody is named, nobody approves, and the work stalls or slips through.

3. Set limits in the systems, not only on paper

The safest rule is one the software cannot break. Give the digital executive read access where reading is enough. Do not give it the ability to release payments or change bank details at all. Keep the approval step inside the tool your team already uses, so it shows up where people look.

4. Keep a record of what it did

Every action should leave a trail: what it read, what it drafted, who approved it and when. That record is how you catch a mistake early, and it is what you will want if a customer, an auditor or your accountant asks a question later.

5. Review the lines after the first month

Once you have seen a month of its work, you may move a task from "prepares" to "does alone," or the other way. Make that change on purpose, write it down, and never move anything from the never list without talking to the people who would answer for it.

Questions to ask anyone who builds this for you

Whether you build it yourself or hire someone, these questions show quickly whether control has been thought through:

  • Which actions can it take without a person approving them, and can I see that list?
  • Can it move money or change payment details? If so, why, and how is that stopped?
  • Where do approvals happen, and who gets notified?
  • What does it log, and can I read the log myself?
  • How do I switch it off, today, without calling anyone?
  • Where does my data go, and who else can see it?

If the answers are vague, the setup is not ready, however impressive the demo looks.

A digital executive should make your decisions faster and better informed. It should never make them for you.
