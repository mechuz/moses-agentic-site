Teach me, build it, or do it for me: choosing your way in

Most owners we meet have already decided that AI belongs somewhere in their business. The harder question is how to bring it in.

November 18, 2026 By Moses Agentic AI agents for business ai consulting for small business

Most owners we meet have already decided that AI belongs somewhere in their business. The harder question is how to bring it in. Do you learn it yourself and teach your team? Do you have someone build it and then run it in-house? Or do you hand the whole thing to a partner who builds it, runs it and reports back? Moses Agentic offers all three, so this article is not a case for one of them. It is the conversation we have in the first ten minutes of every consultation, written down.

Where most businesses stand right now

Adoption is real but uneven. In the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey, 19.8 percent of U.S. businesses reported using AI in any business function as of May 3, 2026 (published May 26, 2026). Use climbed among firms with 20 or more employees over the previous six months and did not change much among smaller ones. Fewer than one in five businesses with four or fewer employees reported using AI at all. Information (39.7 percent) and finance and insurance (33.9 percent) lead; retail trade sits near 14 percent.

The Federal Reserve reached a similar picture from three different surveys. Its April 3, 2026 note on monitoring AI adoption put firm-level adoption at about 18 percent at the end of 2025, with larger firms adopting fastest and professional services and finance out in front. One more number from that note matters for an owner: the share of firms that said they planned to adopt AI within six months rose from about 9 percent at the start of 2025 to more than 20 percent heading into 2026.

Read together, the surveys say two things. If you run a small business, you are not behind; most of your peers have not started. And the ones who have started are concentrated in paperwork-heavy work: analysis, documents, correspondence, numbers. That is also where the three ways to bring AI in differ most.

Way one: teach me

You and your team learn to use AI tools yourselves. Nobody builds anything for you. You sit through training, practice on your own work, and decide what to keep.

Who it fits

Owners who like to understand a thing before they depend on it. Teams of two to ten where one curious person can carry the rest. Businesses whose work changes week to week, so a fixed system would be out of date by the time it was finished.

What it costs you

Time more than money. Our education arm, Chocham AI, runs hands-on seminars that start with the basics and end with you building a working assistant of your own; details and prices are on chochamai.com. The real cost is the hours your people spend practicing, and the discipline to keep practicing after the class ends.

Where it goes wrong

Training without a project. People leave a seminar excited and go back to the same inbox. The fix is to pick one recurring task before the training starts: the weekly vendor follow-up, the monthly numbers summary, the customer questions that arrive by email. Learn on that task, measure that task.

Way two: build it, I run it

A partner designs and builds the system, connects it to your tools, trains your people, then hands over the keys. You operate it day to day. You call for help when something changes.

Who it fits

Businesses with an office manager, a controller or an operations lead who already owns the process being improved. Owners who want control of their data and their vendors. Companies with 10 to 200 people, where one well-built routine touches many hands.

What you are really buying

Judgment, mostly. The build itself is a few weeks of work; the valuable part is choosing what the system should do on its own, what it should draft for a person to approve, and what it must never touch. A good build comes with those rules written down, a short manual, and a person on your side trained to run it.

Where it goes wrong

Handover without an owner. If nobody inside the company is named as the person who runs the thing, it drifts within a month. Name the owner before the build starts, and put a fifteen-minute weekly check on their calendar.

Way three: do it for me

The partner builds the system, runs it, watches it and keeps improving it. You review the results and make the decisions. Think of it as adding a digital workforce that reports to you, with a human team behind it who answer for the work.

Who it fits

Owners who are already stretched thin and know it. Businesses without a natural internal owner for the process. Functions that run on a schedule and produce something reviewable: marketing, bookkeeping support, lead follow-up, reporting.

What stays with you

Every decision that spends money, changes a price, signs a contract or speaks for the company in a way you have not approved. A well-run service draws that line on day one and shows you, in writing, what it did each week. You should be able to read the log in five minutes and know exactly what happened.

Where it goes wrong

Vague scope. "Handle our marketing" is not a job description, for a person or for a digital worker. "Publish two sourced articles a week, answer every review within a day, put every lead in the tracker within the hour" is. Insist on the second kind before anything starts.

A short way to decide

  • Is there one person inside the business who wants to own this and has two hours a week for it? If yes, lean toward teach me or build it, I run it. If no, lean toward do it for me.
  • Does the work change every week? Teach me. Does it repeat on a schedule? Build it, or have it run for you.
  • Does the task touch customer data, money or compliance? Then whichever way you choose, write down what the system may decide alone before it touches anything. For a plain starting point on protecting data, NIST published a cybersecurity guide for owner-only businesses on November 2, 2026 that fits a small team just as well.
  • Can you measure the task today? Count minutes, count errors, count days late. If you cannot measure it, start with a smaller task you can.

Mixing the three

Most businesses end up with a blend, and that is fine. A common pattern: the owner takes a seminar so the vocabulary is familiar, one routine is built and handed to the office manager, and marketing is run as a service because nobody in the building wanted it. Another: a firm starts with do it for me to see results quickly, then brings the routine in-house once a staff member has watched it run for a quarter.

The order matters less than the first step being small, measured and owned by someone. The Census and Federal Reserve numbers above describe businesses that got past that first step. Nothing in them says you need to be large to do it.

What to bring to a first conversation

You do not need a plan. Bring three things: the task that eats the most hours for the least value, the person who does it today, and the tools it lives in (your email, your spreadsheet, your booking system, your accounting software). With those, a thirty-minute conversation is enough to tell you which of the three ways fits, what it would involve, and what it would cost. If the honest answer is "learn it yourself first," you should hear that too.
